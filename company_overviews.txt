Commercial Space Tourism: Company Overviews

Orbital Horizon Aerospace was founded in 2011 and was the first private company to carry paying passengers past the Karman line, achieving this milestone in 2019. The company operates a reusable suborbital capsule called the Meridian, which launches vertically and lands under parachutes. Orbital Horizon's flights last approximately eleven minutes from liftoff to landing, with roughly four minutes of weightlessness once the capsule crosses the boundary of space.

Aurelia Spaceworks takes a different approach, using a winged spaceplane that is carried to high altitude by a jet-powered mothership before igniting its own rocket motor. This horizontal launch method allows Aurelia to operate from conventional runways rather than a dedicated vertical launch pad. Aurelia Spaceworks began commercial passenger flights in 2023 after a decade of test flights and two fatal accidents during development, in 2014 and 2016, both of which led to redesigns of the vehicle's feathering re-entry system.

Halcyon Voyages is the newest entrant in the suborbital tourism market, having completed its first crewed test flight in 2022. Unlike its competitors, Halcyon uses a high-altitude balloon to lift a pressurized capsule to the edge of the stratosphere, offering passengers several hours of gradual ascent and panoramic views of the Earth's curvature, though the capsule does not cross the internationally recognized boundary of space and passengers do not experience weightlessness.

For orbital tourism, Zenith Orbital Systems has flown private citizens to the International Space Station under commercial arrangements brokered with national space agencies. Zenith does not build its own launch vehicles; instead, it purchases seats on existing orbital spacecraft and manages training, medical screening, and mission logistics for its clients. Zenith's orbital missions typically last between eight and fourteen days.
